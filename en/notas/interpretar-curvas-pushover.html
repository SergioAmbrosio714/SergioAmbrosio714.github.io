Pushover: read the mechanism before the point

Capacity, demand and the limits of a static representation.

Interpret stiffness, yielding and displacement demand without treating a graphical intersection as a guarantee of performance.

Introduction

A pushover curve relates base shear to the displacement of a control point during a sequence of lateral loading. It helps reveal changes in global response as inelastic mechanisms develop. On its own, however, the curve does not show where damage concentrates, which connection governs, or whether a brittle component reaches its limit before the rest of the structure. It should therefore be read alongside the progression of component deformations and forces.

The problem: capacity and demand are different quantities

Capacity comes from the structural model and the selected loading pattern. Demand depends on the hazard, the spectrum and the chosen procedure. FEMA 440 (2005) examines improvements to nonlinear static displacement and equivalent-linearization procedures [1]. A performance point is an estimate consistent with those assumptions; it is neither an unchanging building property nor an automatic safety certificate.

Capacity and demand must both be converted into spectral acceleration and spectral displacement before comparison in ADRS coordinates, as described by CSI [2]. Intersecting a spectrum expressed in g with a curve expressed in kN and mm without this transformation has no physical meaning.

Development: three complementary readings

First, inspect the initial branch: its slope depends on effective stiffness, support conditions and the representation of cracked regions. Next, identify the yield idealization and the evolution of tangent stiffness. Finally, investigate strength loss, instability and changes in the governing mechanism. A monotonic envelope does not fully describe cyclic degradation; FEMA P-440A (2009) addresses this distinction explicitly [4].

Displacement control prescribes increments at a selected degree of freedom and can follow part of a post-elastic response path [3]. A converged increment meets the adopted numerical criteria; local demands must still be checked. Conversely, a terminated run does not establish collapse at its final completed increment without further investigation.

K₀ = Vᵧ/δᵧ; Ksec = V/δ; μ = δ/δᵧ

Conceptual example that can be checked by hand

Consider a fictional bilinear relationship with Vᵧ = 500 kN, δᵧ = 20 mm and a post-yield slope of 2 kN/mm. The initial stiffness is K₀ = 25 kN/mm. At an assumed displacement of 60 mm, V = 580 kN, Ksec ≈ 9.67 kN/mm and μ = 3. These values describe only the stated idealized relationship.

The 60 mm displacement was not obtained from a response spectrum and must not be labelled a performance point. Estimating such a point additionally requires seismic demand, a modal transformation and a compatibility procedure. Evaluating acceptance also requires component demands and applicable limits.

V(δ) = 25δ for δ ≤ 20 mm; V(δ) = 500 + 2(δ − 20) for δ > 20 mm

A reproducible check of the example

Before processing an exported curve, check a known relationship. This snippet reproduces only the three example values; it does not find a performance point. A real series would retain the increment, control displacement and base reaction with their signs, units and converged state. Response branches with reversals require examining the solution path rather than blindly sorting rows by displacement.

from math import isclose

vy_kn, dy_mm, kpost_kn_mm = 500.0, 20.0, 2.0
d_mm = 60.0
v_kn = vy_kn + kpost_kn_mm * (d_mm - dy_mm)
ksec_kn_mm = v_kn / d_mm
mu = d_mm / dy_mm
assert isclose(v_kn, 580.0)
assert isclose(ksec_kn_mm, 29.0 / 3.0)
assert isclose(mu, 3.0)

Common mistakes

  • Comparing curves without keeping weight, control point, units and lateral-load pattern consistent.
  • Treating a higher peak force as a universal improvement in ductility or deformation capacity.
  • Ignoring torsion, higher modes, P–Δ effects or force-controlled components when relevant.
  • Assigning automatic hinges without checking materials, sections, axial interaction and applicability.

Conclusion and scope

A useful review links the shape of the curve to the resisting mechanism and identifiable local demands. The record should include assumptions, loading pattern, control method, tolerances and sensitivity checks. This educational analytical example is not an analysis of a built project and does not prescribe project acceptance criteria.
