Linear or nonlinear: let the question decide

Model complexity should follow the behavior that matters.

Material response, geometry and contact can change the solution. A bilinear example shows when elastic extrapolation stops being valid.

Introduction

Choosing linear or nonlinear analysis begins with a question: what response does the project need to establish? Service displacement, redistribution after yielding and loss of stability require different assumptions. Adding parameters does not guarantee a better answer. It does require the analyst to explain which mechanism each parameter represents and what evidence supports it.

What a linear model assumes

In linear statics, a constant stiffness matrix relates forces and displacements. Proportionality allows scaling and superposition within those assumptions. This does not make every software output directly additive: modal spectral maxima, for example, require an appropriate combination rule and are not a simultaneous response history.

A load pattern must also be distinguished from an analysis case. CSI explains that the pattern defines actions, while the case determines how they are applied and how response is calculated [3]. Correctly drawn geometry with an incorrectly defined case still solves a different problem from the intended one.

K u = F; K is constant within the linear assumptions

What changes in nonlinear analysis

Material nonlinearity occurs when constitutive response changes with state, as in plasticity, cracking or degradation. Geometric nonlinearity includes the influence of the deformed configuration on equilibrium. Opening and closing contact can change restraints. These mechanisms may coexist, but they should be introduced and checked separately.

An incremental Newton solution corrects displacements using the imbalance between external and internal forces. OpenSees documentation describes the role of tangent stiffness in this iteration [2]. Nonlinearity does not always involve large displacements; an elastic structure may also exhibit significant geometric effects.

NIST GCR 17-917-46v1 (2017) provides a framework for organizing assumptions, parameters and checks in nonlinear models [1]. One practical approach is to first recover elastic response at low demand, then examine sensitivity to stiffness, strength and support assumptions.

r(u) = Fext − Fint(u); Kt(uᵢ) Δuᵢ = r(uᵢ)

Conceptual example: two springs, one force

Consider two fictional springs with an initial stiffness of 10 kN/mm. The first remains linear. The second yields at 100 kN and then follows a positive slope of 1 kN/mm. Under a static force of 120 kN, the linear model gives 12 mm; the bilinear model gives 10 + (120 − 100)/1 = 30 mm.

The difference comes from the selected constitutive relationship, rather than automatic superiority of the second model. If that relationship does not represent the physical component, 30 mm is not a valid prediction either. This calculation defines neither unloading nor cyclic dissipation, fatigue or fracture; those behaviors would require further assumptions.

An elastic benchmark for model checks

The downloadable cantilever adds a proportionality check. With fixed section and material, δ = PL³/(3EI): doubling P doubles δ; doubling L multiplies it by eight. For a rectangular section, doubling h multiplies I by eight and divides δ by eight, with b unchanged. Starting from 2 mm gives 4, 16 and 0.25 mm, respectively.

These relationships help identify conversion errors or unintended changes in assumptions. They follow Euler–Bernoulli theory, which omits shear deformation: doubling h in this example changes L/h from 10 to 5, requiring review of that idealization. Loss of proportionality in a nonlinear model must be explained by its mechanism rather than corrected by forcing this benchmark.

Common mistakes

  • Applying superposition to nonlinear states with different load histories or contact conditions.
  • Confusing equilibrium convergence with experimental validation or mesh independence.
  • Relaxing tolerances or adding stabilization without checking units, mechanisms and balances.
  • Changing the solver to hide an incorrect restraint: contact and equilibrium problems require a physical diagnosis [4].

Conclusion and scope

An appropriate model is the simplest one that preserves the mechanisms needed for the decision. Comparisons should record which assumptions change and how those changes affect the quantities of interest. This is an educational analytical example, not a project design.
