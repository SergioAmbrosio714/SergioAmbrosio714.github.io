Retrofit starts by explaining the deficiency

Diagnosis, performance objectives and system compatibility.

Local strengthening changes the whole structure. Choosing jackets, FRP or new resisting elements depends on the mechanism and the available evidence.

Introduction

An intervention should not begin by selecting a strengthening material. First establish the deficiency, why it matters and what improvement is required. Section loss, inadequate deformation capacity, discontinuous load paths and deteriorated substrate are different problems. A technique effective for one may be irrelevant to another or transfer demand to a component that was not previously examined.

The problem: establish the as-built condition

Assessment should distinguish documented, observed, tested and inferred information. Original drawings, changes in use, previous repairs, measured geometry and material condition inform a model with explicit uncertainty. A strength value stated in a design document does not automatically establish a confirmed property of the existing structure.

ASCE/SEI 41-23 organizes seismic evaluation and retrofit through performance objectives and evaluation tiers [1]. ACI CODE-562-25 addresses assessment, repair and rehabilitation of existing concrete [2]. Their scopes differ: the applicable framework must be established without simply transferring new-building requirements to an assessment by analogy.

Development: from mechanism to intervention

Diagnosis should identify the limitation: strength, stability, deformation, load transfer or durability. Alternatives can then be compared against the objective and construction sequence. A jacket can change dimensions, stiffness and force transfer; additional walls or braces change global distribution. Diaphragms, connections and foundations therefore also need review.

An FRP system can contribute to particular strengthening or confinement mechanisms, but fiber orientation, anchorage, bond, concrete condition and exposure must be defined. ACI PRC-440.2-23 is a guide for externally bonded FRP systems [3]; its existence does not make every wrap a valid solution. Greater strength does not automatically restore initial stiffness or resolve corrosion.

For existing steel structures, AISC 342-22 provides a specialized seismic evaluation and retrofit framework [4]. In every material, brittle mechanisms and deformation compatibility need examination. Strengthening a member provides little benefit if its connection or support cannot receive the resulting demand.

Conceptual example: strengthening redistributes force

Idealize a floor with two parallel lateral systems and a rigid diaphragm without torsion. If both systems have stiffness k and an imposed horizontal force of 100 kN acts on the floor, each receives 50 kN. Increasing only the first stiffness to 3k changes the distribution to 75 kN and 25 kN.

This demonstrates displacement compatibility and equilibrium, not the effectiveness of an actual intervention. Total force has been held artificially constant: in seismic analysis, changing stiffness may also change periods and demand. A favorable assessment would require reviewing strength, deformation, connections and foundations under the revised response.

F₁ = [k₁/(k₁ + k₂)] F; F₂ = [k₂/(k₁ + k₂)] F

Common mistakes

  • Selecting a solution before recording damage, materials, geometry and the performance objective.
  • Comparing nominal strengths alone while overlooking redistribution or construction-stage conditions.
  • Assuming that every model input has the same degree of certainty.
  • Presenting lower demand in one member as a system-wide improvement without checking where the force moved.

Conclusion and scope

A proposal should connect evidence, diagnosis, objectives, alternatives and verification, with documented uncertainty and a construction-stage control strategy. Final review checks the modified system and its compatibility, not just the strengthened component.
